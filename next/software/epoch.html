Epoch

A working environment for one person and their AI collaborators, built on one rule: the conversation is the record, and everything you read is drawn from it.

The problem

Most knowledge tools treat the document as the thing worth keeping and the conversation as throwaway. But for a person who thinks by talking, and who now does much of that talking with an AI, the thinking is in the conversations, and it is lost in them. After two years of daily use you cannot find what you decided, you re-explain the same background to every new assistant, and the tools you use to keep track become another thing to keep track of. Most AI memory products fetch the best matching snippets when you ask. That is useful, but it does not give you a picture of your own work that stays current, checks itself, and that you and your AI collaborators both read from.

Who it is for

Epoch is built by and for one person: an entrepreneur and solo operator with ADHD, who works in high-variety ways and wants her tools to fit her, not the reverse. That is the first user. The same design is meant to suit other founders, researchers, artists and consultants who work heavily with AI and have lots of context to keep. It is not a company brain, and it is not made for teams of hundreds.

How it works

  1. Every conversation with an AI, from every source, is captured and stored unchanged. Today that is several sources and well over a hundred thousand turns.
  2. Each source is read by its own small parser and passed through one checked, logged entry point, so that nothing enters the record any other way.
  3. From the record, the system builds a catalogue of what exists, a graph of how things connect, and a searchable index that works by meaning as well as keywords.
  4. Wiki pages, calendars, boards, maps and views are drawn from that record. They are disposable: if one is wrong or out of date, it is redrawn, and the record is untouched.
  5. Named AI agents each look after one area, such as the calendar, the contacts, the database or the design. They read widely, write only through the checked entry points, and leave their work where the next agent or person can pick it up.
  6. The person works in one app, shaped for how they work, where all of this is reachable.

What exists today

Epoch is in daily use at Mangrove, and Rosie is its only user at the moment. The record holds about two years of her conversations with AI, from eight different sources, and is searchable by meaning. Around it sit a contacts and organisations register, a catalogue of what exists and who owns it, and a team of named agents, each with a defined area. The app she works in has a calm day view, and it is being built as a spatial, three-dimensional building whose rooms hold the work and the agents who look after it. Further parts are designed and being built in turn: a calendar of our own, an asynchronous shared space where agents ask each other questions and Rosie can read and join, and a shared board beside each working session. Epoch is not sold, and it is not a database, a graph product or an agent-memory layer. Those parts are standard and are the means, not what is special about it.

What comes next

The next step is the building: a spatial entrance where each room holds a part of the work and the agent who looks after it, with the same map available as a flat picture. Alongside it, the calendar, the shared space for agents and the session board are being completed, and Mangrove's own products are being moved onto Epoch's pipeline, starting with the way Mindsweeper reads an archive. The longer aim is a version that others can use for their own conversations and organisations.
