Choreotope (name open)

Tilt a phone, move in front of a camera, or play music, and a flock of birds moves and makes sound in answer.

The problem

A flock of starlings looks choreographed, and nobody is directing it. Each bird follows a few simple rules about its neighbours, and the shape of the whole flock appears from that. It is a good picture of how a group can move together without a conductor. The invitation here is to put a person inside that: what happens to the flock when a body, a phone or a piece of music can steer it? For people who make dance and music it is also a new way to sketch movement, because you play it and watch it respond.

Who it is for

Choreographers and dancers who want to sketch with movement, musicians and live visual artists who want visuals that answer a performance, and audiences who can join in with their own phones.

How it works

  1. Each bird in the flock follows simple local rules: stay close to your neighbours, line up with them, do not collide. This is the boids model, published by Craig Reynolds in 1987.
  2. An input turns your movement into forces on the flock: the tilt of a phone, your movement seen by the camera, a dancer's body, a piece of music.
  3. The same flock also makes sound, so what you do is both seen and heard.
  4. One engine sits behind every version, with one list of settings, so a way of playing can be saved as a preset and recalled.
  5. Several phones can join one session, and you can capture stills and video.
  6. A notation view writes movement down in the way ballet does, using Benesh notation, and can drive both a figure and the flock. The camera and sensor data stay on your device.

What exists today

Three pages play in a browser today. Tilt Flock turns a phone into a kind of theremin. Dance Flock reads camera movement and has a movement loop pedal. Engine is one flock and sound engine that takes several inputs and links a phone to a screen. We tested it on an iPad on 29 Sep 2026. Behind them is a full application with a three-dimensional flock, a figure and sculpting stage, a Benesh score and frame view, saved scenes and tests. Before this, about eight months of engine variants, nine in all, were built and shown on a live site, including versions driven by a musician's recordings and by position sensors worn on the body. The notation work is still at the design stage, and the animation code from an earlier attempt at Benesh was not recovered. We have not yet decided whether to release it as open source or sell it.

What comes next

Rosie has asked for a calmer, simpler version, and that comes first: fewer controls, a quieter look, and a new name. After that, working with a dancer to test it in a rehearsal room, then other natural systems such as calving icebergs and sheepdogs herding, a movement score that drives the flock, and a performance view for live visuals. Whether it is released openly or sold is still to be decided.
