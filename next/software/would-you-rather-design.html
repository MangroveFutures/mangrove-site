Would You Rather: chores edition

Everyone in the house sorts the chores into hate, meh and love, then turns the cards over together.

The problem

In most homes the mental load is carried unevenly, and a lot of it goes unseen. Chores usually get discussed when everyone is already tired and irritated, so the talk turns into who did what and who is to blame, and resentment builds. People also differ more than they say: a chore one person finds dull can be distressing to someone else, because of noise, smell or texture, and nobody has ever put that on the table. The game gives a household a calm moment to find out what each person actually enjoys, dreads and would avoid at any cost, before anyone has to argue about it.

Who it is for

Households first, and especially households with neurodivergent members: it is built for them from the start, with positive framing, no points or shame, and nicknames rather than real names. The same idea carries over later to classrooms and to teams at work.

How it works

  1. Each person sorts the chore cards on their own into three piles: hate it, meh, or love it.
  2. They add detail on a sticker map on the fridge: what exactly makes a chore bad or good for them.
  3. The household sits down and turns the cards over together, so everyone's piles are visible at once.
  4. They talk about it: who already likes what, what could be shared or swapped, and what would make a dreaded chore possible, such as different timing, different tools or doing it together. The aim is a conversation that begins from how each person feels, not from who has been doing too little.

What exists today

You can try a first version in a web browser: a single page where you swipe through the cards on your own and it remembers your answers on your device. It was published on 5 Oct 2026 and informed by interviews with two households. A print-at-home version is designed. Saving a whole household's answers together, the table reveal across several people, and a polished printable deck are not built yet, and we have not run a full household test. A separate game of the same name is used inside Mangrove to learn Rosie's design taste, by showing her pairs of designs and asking which she prefers and why. It shares the method but is not the chores game.

What comes next

Next is a test with a real household, to see whether the conversation it opens is the calm one we are aiming for. After that, saving a household's answers together, a print-at-home deck, and School and Work editions that reuse the same method. Whether to lead with print or with the browser, and whether to keep the name, are still to be decided.
