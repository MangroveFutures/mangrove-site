SketchIn

Photograph a hand-drawn page and SketchIn keeps it, reads it, redraws it cleanly and files it where you can find it again.

The problem

Many people think by drawing. The drawing ends up in a notebook or a camera roll and goes no further. Rosie put it this way on 14 April 2026: she likes sketching by hand but rarely does anything with it.

The sketch is often the first form of an idea. You are working out a shape, a flow or a mental model, and you cannot yet explain it to anyone. Typing it up first defeats the point.

Reading handwriting and diagrams by machine is also harder than it looks. In 2025 Rosie tried twice to get a general chat tool to read photographed pages and a screenshot of a whiteboard map. Both attempts timed out or came back incomplete, and she had to ask three times for the full list from one set of 21 pages. SketchIn exists because that job needs a pipeline of its own.

Who it is for

People who think by drawing: neurodivergent thinkers, creatives and knowledge workers who sketch to work something out and want the sketch to count.

Teams who want to see how an organisation really works. Each person draws their part, where the data flows and where it gets stuck, and the drawings are combined into one map that shows the bottlenecks.

Anyone who wants to keep control of their own material. You can download everything and connect it to your other tools.

How it works

  1. Photograph. Take a picture of the page with a phone or a document camera, or drop the file in a watched folder.
  2. Keep the original. The photograph is stored unchanged, with a record of where it came from, and is always there to go back to at full size.
  3. Read. The words are transcribed as written, and the page is described in plain text. This first pass makes no interpretation.
  4. Ask. If you want, SketchIn asks questions so you can say in your own words what the drawing means. How many questions depends on how confident or tired you are.
  5. Redraw. You get a clean vector copy, true to the original.
  6. Interpret. SketchIn compares the sketch with the reference images you have approved and with what the project already knows, then writes an interpretation.
  7. Suggest forms. It names established diagram types your sketch resembles, such as a directed graph or a Voronoi diagram, and shows your own sketch redrawn in that form next to a worked example. It also points to existing words and frameworks so you do not have to invent your own.
  8. File and link. The sketch goes into your sketchbook, searchable by meaning and linked to related sketches and ideas. Sketches can be nested, and split into several drawings by scale.
  9. Learn you. Over time it keeps a list of the visual forms you approve and builds a picture of how you think.

What exists today

SketchIn is being developed for Rosie's own work first. Here is what exists as of 20 November 2026.

A script reads one photographed sketch with a vision model and writes four things: a transcription with marks where it is unsure, a description, the concepts the page refers to, and a confidence note. It has been run on Rosie's own sketches since 22 May 2026.

46 of her hand-drawn pages have been fetched from a shared folder, described and registered, by hand, on 6 July 2026.

A cataloguing pipeline now runs the same steps (register, snapshot, describe, embed, link) over 1,343 of Mangrove's own design artifacts, with a stable identifier and content hash for each. That is the most recent use of the pipeline, on a different kind of material, and it works.

What is not built yet: automatic capture from a phone or folder, the redraw, the questions, the diagram suggestions, links to the wider knowledge graph, and the sketchbook screen. Today you would run the reading step yourself, one image at a time.

What comes next

The order is fixed. First, the backlog of sketches registered. Then one sketch taken end to end through the pipeline. Then a watched folder that picks up new photographs and prepares them for review without changing anything. Then the sketchbook screen and a packaged version for other people.

A sketch counts as done when you can photograph it and upload it, find it in a real sketchbook, not a folder, and be sure agents can find it later and know what you meant. You can always find it again, you learn something from it (a connection, or an established diagram style or term), and it is never again a scrap of paper on the desk.

Client sketches and anyone's data other than Rosie's will have their own rules for keeping them apart before they go through it.
