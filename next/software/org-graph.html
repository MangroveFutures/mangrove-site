Org graph

One connected picture of your people, tools, documents, meetings and decisions, that you can slice, zoom and read.

The problem

An org chart shows who reports to whom. It does not show how work and knowledge actually move. In most organisations the real working knowledge sits with a few people and is spread across many tools that do not talk to each other. If one of those people is ill for a day or goes on holiday, things fall apart, and nobody can say beforehand which person that would be. The same is true of a technical estate: dozens of apps, databases, scripts and scheduled jobs, and no single place that shows how they fit.

Who it is for

Small and mid-sized organisations and charities that want to see how they really work, and are preparing for change: someone retiring, a team growing, a handover. It is also for technical teams who want one picture of their whole system rather than a folder of separate diagrams.

How it works

  1. We gather what already exists: people, clients, tools and subscriptions, documents, calendar entries, meeting records and decisions.
  2. Each item becomes a node in one graph, and the links between them become typed connections: who talks to whom, who owns what, what depends on what, where the evidence came from.
  3. We draw any slice you ask for: one team, one client, one tool, one decision. A deliverable is a question asked of the graph, not a separate copy that goes out of date.
  4. You read it beside your org chart. The differences are the finding: the people holding knowledge alone, the tools nobody owns, the paths that run round the official structure.
  5. For a handover, the same graph answers "what does this person know and do, and who needs to be told". It works without a full graph and gets better as the graph fills in.

What exists today

The idea has been tried for real on one client's organisation, in a pilot over July 2026, which held 46 meetings and about 4,700 conversation turns and had a client space built for it. Mangrove's own estate is the worked example, drawn as the Mother of All Diagrams, the single map of everything Mangrove runs. On 24 Oct 2026 we built five design directions for it. The leading one, Atlas, is a self-contained map of thirteen islands on seven shelves, with zoom from overview to detail, a mode that highlights gaps, and a presenting mode that hides private text. Atlas is a prototype working from a snapshot, not live data. The importer that builds the graph automatically from each system's own description is not built yet.

What comes next

Next is building the graph automatically from each system's own architecture description, so the map follows the work instead of being redrawn by hand, and showing what is running now beside what is only planned. We are also deciding what a client receives at the end of an engagement: our current thinking is a one-time piece of work after which the client owns the result, rather than a hosted service. Two short films are planned, one explaining the idea to each audience and one showing it on real data.
